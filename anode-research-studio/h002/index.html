The thesis

Delegation is not the transfer of a vote. It is the transfer of discretion.

Delegation is usually described as a mechanism for moving voting power from one holder to another. That description is technically correct and institutionally incomplete. What actually moves is the ability to interpret information, exercise judgment and act within a mandate.

This handbook looks at delegated discretion across token delegates, DReps, validators, councils, stewards, custodians and emerging software agents.

Why it matters

Delegation is becoming an institutional governance layer. Different intermediaries exercise radically different kinds of authority, yet many systems measure them using the same blunt proxies: participation, visible activity and voting output.

That becomes more problematic as AI makes summaries, rationales and proposal analysis dramatically cheaper to produce. The hard problem shifts from output production to the evaluation of judgment.

Selected findings

01

Equal voice does not imply equal context.

Governance rights may be symmetrical while information, expertise and attention remain unevenly distributed.

02

Transparency is not accountability.

Public rationales improve observability, but accountability requires someone to evaluate behavior and change the state of the relationship.

03

Delegation is a cost transfer.

The principal saves attention and analysis, but inherits new costs: selecting the agent, defining the mandate, monitoring conflicts and replacing them when necessary.

04

Low participation is not a diagnosis.

Attention, expertise, quorum and operational latency are different problems. They should not automatically receive the same delegation architecture.

05

AI scales output, not responsibility.

Governance systems increasingly need ways to distinguish generated activity from accountable human or institutional judgment.

Delegation architecture

A useful delegation system can be decomposed into five questions:

  1. Authority source: where does the delegated mandate originate?
  2. Scope: which decisions or domains are included?
  3. Discretion: how much independent judgment can the intermediary exercise?
  4. Execution: can the intermediary recommend, vote, transact or otherwise act?
  5. Revocation: how can authority be constrained, replaced or withdrawn?
Delegation is not abandonment. Good architecture preserves a path back to the principal.

AI and responsibility

AI changes the economics of governance work. Many of the visible artifacts that governance programs reward can now be produced faster and at lower cost. That is useful, but it weakens output volume as a proxy for judgment quality.

The emerging question is not whether AI participates in governance. It already does. The question is where final authority sits, how decisions are verified and who is accountable when delegated systems fail.
